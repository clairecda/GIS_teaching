# Week 12 Facilitator Notes: Capstone Showcase & Course Wrap-up

## Session Overview

**Duration:** 3 hours (adjust based on class size)

**Learning Objectives:**
By the end of this session, students will be able to:
1. Present their capstone project clearly, explaining their research question, methods, and findings to a non-technical audience
2. Organize project files in a logical, professional structure that facilitates future use and sharing
3. Give and receive constructive peer feedback on spatial analysis work
4. Reflect meaningfully on their learning journey and identify concrete next steps for continued GIS practice

**Session Type:** Capstone presentations, peer feedback, and course closure

**Materials Needed:**
- [ ] Presentation setup (projector, screen sharing capability, audio check)
- [ ] Timer or stopwatch (visible to presenter and audience)
- [ ] Printed peer feedback forms (or digital forms ready to share)
- [ ] Student roster with presentation order
- [ ] Recording equipment (if recording presentations for portfolios)
- [ ] Certificate of completion templates (if applicable)
- [ ] Course evaluation forms
- [ ] Celebratory refreshments (if budget allows)
- [ ] Whiteboard/flip chart for group reflection activity
- [ ] Backup plan for technical difficulties (offline slides, backup projector)

**Room Setup:**
- Arrange seating to foster community (circle, U-shape, or theater style facing presentation screen)
- Test all technology 30 minutes before class
- Have water available for presenters
- Create a "green room" space if possible for presenters to prepare

## Session Flow

### Opening (15 minutes)

**9:00-9:15 AM** *(adjust times based on your schedule)*

**Welcome and context-setting:**
1. **Acknowledge the moment** (2 min):
   - "You started this course 12 weeks ago, many of you opening QGIS for the first time. Today you'll present spatial analyses that combine multiple datasets, sophisticated techniques, and real-world applications. Take a moment to recognize how far you've come."

2. **Set the tone** (3 min):
   - This is a celebration, not an exam
   - Every project represents significant learning, even if imperfect
   - The goal is to share insights and support each other
   - Emphasize the growth mindset: these are "version 1.0" projects that can evolve

3. **Review presentation format** (5 min):
   - 8-10 minutes presentation
   - 2-3 minutes Q&A
   - Time warnings at 7 minutes and 9 minutes
   - Peer feedback form expectations
   - Q&A etiquette (respectful, curious, constructive)

4. **Housekeeping** (5 min):
   - Presentation order
   - Break schedule
   - Where to find feedback forms
   - Recording policy and permissions
   - Bathroom location, refreshment access

**Facilitation tip:** Model enthusiasm and warmth. Your energy sets the tone for how students will engage with each other's work.

---

### Presentation Block 1 (60-75 minutes)

**9:15-10:30 AM**

**Format for each presentation:**
- **Setup (1-2 min):** Presenter connects, loads slides, audience prepares feedback forms
- **Presentation (8-10 min):** Student presents following their prepared structure
- **Q&A (2-3 min):** Facilitated questions from peers and instructor
- **Transition (1-2 min):** Next presenter sets up while audience completes feedback forms

**Number of presentations in Block 1:** 4-5 students (12-15 min per slot)

**Facilitation strategies:**

**Time management:**
- Use a visible timer (projector slide, phone timer shared on screen, or physical countdown)
- Give gentle time warnings: "You have 3 minutes remaining" at the 7-minute mark, "1 minute to wrap up" at 9 minutes
- If a student goes over 10 minutes, gently interrupt: "Let's pause here and move to questions so we can hear from everyone today"
- Build buffer time between presentations for technical transitions

**During presentations:**
- Take your own notes to model engaged listening
- Make eye contact and nod to encourage nervous presenters
- If a presenter stumbles, give them a moment to recover before offering help
- Note which students struggle with time management for individual feedback later

**Facilitating Q&A:**
- Start with: "What questions do we have for [Student Name]?"
- If silence, have a prepared question ready: "I'm curious about [specific aspect of their methodology]—can you say more about that choice?"
- Invite peer questions: "Did anyone notice an interesting pattern in the maps?"
- Redirect overly technical or critical questions: "That's a great direction for future work. What would you explore if you had more time, [Student Name]?"
- End each Q&A with specific praise: "The way you framed the equity dimension of this analysis was really compelling. Thank you."

**Handling technical difficulties:**
- Have a backup plan: student can present from PDF, from your computer, or verbally walk through their project
- Stay calm and positive: "Technology happens. Let's use this minute to stretch while we sort this out."
- If recording fails, offer to record a makeup session later
- Don't let tech issues derail the celebration

**Encouraging peer engagement:**
- If audience attention lags, take a 2-minute stand-and-stretch break
- Prompt specific feedback: "As you watch, notice one design choice that works really well in their maps"
- Celebrate good questions from peers: "Excellent question—that's the kind of critical thinking that makes analysis stronger"

---

### Break (15 minutes)

**10:30-10:45 AM**

- Encourage students to move, refresh, chat informally
- This is a good time for presenters who went first to decompress
- Check in with upcoming presenters to ensure they're ready

---

### Presentation Block 2 (60-75 minutes)

**10:45 AM-12:00 PM**

**Format:** Same as Block 1

**Number of presentations in Block 2:** 4-5 students

**Mid-session check:**
- If energy is flagging, add a brief (2-minute) energizer or reflection prompt between presentations
- Example: "In the chat, share one thing that surprised you in the presentations so far"
- Adjust timing if running behind—trim Q&A slightly rather than cutting presentations short

**Facilitation tip:** Notice which presentation techniques work well (clear maps, compelling storytelling, good pacing) and verbally acknowledge them. This teaches presenting skills in real-time.

---

### Lunch Break (Optional, 30-45 minutes)

**12:00-12:45 PM**

If your class is longer or has more students, build in a meal break. Use this time to:
- Let students socialize and celebrate informally
- Review remaining presentation order
- Recharge for the afternoon

*Skip this if your class is shorter or if all presentations fit in the morning.*

### Presentation Block 3 (If needed, 60-75 minutes)

**12:45-2:00 PM**

**Format:** Same as previous blocks

**For larger classes:**
- If you have more than 10-12 students, you may need a third presentation block
- Consider splitting the class across multiple days or running parallel sessions with a co-facilitator
- Alternatively, shorten presentation time to 6-7 minutes with 2-minute Q&A for larger cohorts

### Group Reflection & Discussion (30 minutes)

**2:00-2:30 PM** *(adjust based on when presentations end)*

After all presentations are complete, transition to collective reflection.

**Activity: Course Journey Mapping (15 min)**

Use a whiteboard or shared digital space to map the course journey together.

1. **Draw a timeline:** Week 1 → Week 12 across the board
2. **Invite contributions:** "Let's mark the key moments of our journey. Where were the peaks? The valleys? The turning points?"
3. **Prompt specific memories:**
   - Week 1: "Who remembers their first attempt at loading data into QGIS?"
   - Week 5-6: "When did spatial analysis start to click?"
   - Week 9-10: "What was the hardest debugging session you had?"
   - Week 12: "What are you most proud of today?"

4. **Visualize growth:**
   - Use different colors for challenges (red) and breakthroughs (green)
   - Notice patterns: did everyone struggle in Week 4? Did Week 7's Python intro create an aha moment?
   - Celebrate collective resilience

**Activity: Wisdom Sharing (10 min)**

**Prompt:** "If you could send one piece of advice back in time to your Week 1 self, what would it be?"

Go around the room (or use chat for virtual classes) and have each student share one insight. Common themes might include:
- "It's okay not to understand everything immediately"
- "Google and Stack Exchange are your friends"
- "Start assignments early—debugging takes time"
- "The error messages actually contain useful information"
- "Asking for help is a strength, not a weakness"

**Facilitation tip:** Write these down and share them with next year's class as "Wisdom from Previous Students."

**Discussion prompts for remaining time (5 min):**
- What ethical considerations will you carry forward into future spatial work?
- How has your understanding of what GIS can (and can't) do evolved?
- What surprised you most about spatial analysis?

---

### Closing Ceremony (20 minutes)

**2:30-2:50 PM**

This is the moment to make the ending memorable and meaningful.

**1. Acknowledgments (5 min):**
- Thank students for their hard work, risk-taking, and persistence
- Acknowledge specific moments of growth you witnessed
- Highlight the community you built together
- Recognize any guest attendees who came to support students

**2. Continuing the Journey (5 min):**
- Distribute the "Continuing Your GIS Journey" resource handout (see Resources section below)
- Encourage students to set one concrete goal for the next 3 months
- Remind them: "GIS is a practice. Expertise comes from continued use."
- Invite them to stay in touch—share future projects, ask questions, offer support

**3. Certificates/Recognition (5 min):**
- If you have completion certificates, distribute them with individual congratulations
- Consider small awards or shout-outs: "Most Creative Analysis," "Best Map Design," "Most Improved," "Best Question-Asker"
- Keep it light and inclusive—everyone should feel celebrated

**4. Final Words (5 min):**
Offer a closing statement that honors their growth and sends them forward with confidence. Example:

> "Twelve weeks ago, many of you had never opened QGIS. Today you presented spatial analyses that combined multiple datasets, sophisticated methods, and real-world insights. You've learned to think spatially—to ask not just 'what?' and 'why?' but 'where?' and 'how does location matter?'
>
> The projects you shared today aren't perfect, and that's exactly as it should be. You're at the beginning of your GIS journey, not the end. You now have the foundation, the vocabulary, and the troubleshooting skills to keep building.
>
> You're now part of a global community of people who use geography to understand and improve the world. Keep mapping. Keep questioning. Keep sharing what you learn.
>
> Congratulations, and thank you for being such an engaged, curious, supportive cohort. I can't wait to see what you map next."

**5. Course Evaluations (5 min):**
- Distribute course evaluation forms (institutional requirement)
- Emphasize the value of honest feedback for improving future iterations
- Allow time for completion or explain when they're due

**Optional celebration:**
- Take a group photo
- Share refreshments and informal conversation
- Create a "Where are they going next?" board where students post their next steps
- Exchange contact information for staying connected

## Presentation Guidelines for Students

**Share these guidelines in advance and reiterate them at the start of presentations.**

### What Students Should Cover

**Required elements:**
1. **Problem statement:** What question are you answering? Why does it matter?
2. **Geographic context:** Where is your study area? What makes this location significant?
3. **Data sources:** What datasets did you use? (Name, source, year)
4. **Methods:** What GIS techniques did you apply? (buffers, spatial joins, network analysis, etc.)
5. **Key findings:** What did you discover? (2-4 main insights supported by maps/visualizations)
6. **Limitations:** What are the caveats or constraints of your analysis?
7. **Next steps:** What would you explore with more time/data/resources?

**Optional elements:**
- Workflow diagram showing analysis steps
- Quantitative results (tables, charts)
- Comparison of multiple scenarios
- Policy or practice recommendations

### Time Limits

- **Total presentation time:** 8-10 minutes
  - Hook: 30 seconds
  - Problem framing: 1-2 minutes
  - Data & methods: 2-3 minutes
  - Key findings: 3-4 minutes
  - Limitations & next steps: 1-2 minutes

- **Q&A time:** 2-3 minutes

**Time management tips to share:**
- Practice with a timer—multiple times
- Have a "worst case" version ready: if you're running out of time, which slides can you skip?
- Aim for 8 minutes so you have buffer time
- If you go over 10 minutes, the facilitator will gently move you to Q&A

### Presentation Best Practices

**Visual design:**
- One main idea per slide
- Every map should be self-explanatory (title, legend, scale, source)
- Use high-contrast colors (check accessibility with Color Oracle)
- Avoid cluttered slides—white space is good
- Font size minimum: 24pt for body text, 36pt for titles

**Storytelling:**
- Start with the "why"—hook your audience
- Build a narrative arc: question → methods → findings → implications
- Don't show every map you made—curate your 3-5 best visualizations
- Use annotations (arrows, callouts) to guide the viewer's eye
- End with a clear takeaway

**Delivery:**
- Speak to the audience, not the screen
- Pause for emphasis and to let visuals sink in
- Maintain eye contact
- Modulate your voice—avoid monotone
- It's okay to show enthusiasm about your findings
- If you get nervous, take a breath and slow down

**Handling questions:**
- Listen fully before responding
- It's okay to say "I don't know" or "That's a great direction for future work"
- Avoid getting defensive—questions are opportunities to clarify and improve
- If you don't understand a question, ask for clarification

## Assessment Rubric

**Use this rubric to evaluate capstone projects. Share it with students in advance so expectations are clear.**

### Evaluation Criteria

Students are assessed on both the quality of their analysis and their ability to communicate it effectively.

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Problem Framing** | Clear, focused research question with strong justification for why it matters; geographic context is well-established | Clear research question with some justification; geographic context is present | Vague or overly broad question; weak justification for why it matters; limited geographic context | No clear research question; missing justification or context |
| **Data & Methods** | Appropriate data sources identified and used correctly; methods are well-suited to the question; workflow is logical and documented | Appropriate data sources; standard methods applied correctly; workflow is understandable | Data sources or methods have some issues (inappropriate resolution, missing attribution); workflow has gaps | Major data problems (wrong data type, missing sources); methods are misapplied or missing |
| **Technical Execution** | Analysis runs without errors; all code is documented; outputs are high quality and export-ready; file organization is excellent | Analysis works with minor issues; most code is documented; outputs are adequate; file organization is clear | Analysis has errors that affect results; minimal documentation; outputs are incomplete or poor quality; file organization is unclear | Analysis doesn't work; no documentation; outputs are missing; files are chaotic |
| **Spatial Insight** | Findings clearly and compellingly answer the research question; spatial patterns are identified and interpreted thoughtfully; insights go beyond description | Findings address the research question; spatial patterns are identified and interpreted adequately | Findings are tangential to the question; patterns are identified but interpretation is limited; mostly descriptive | No clear findings; patterns are not identified or misinterpreted; no analysis beyond description |
| **Communication** | Maps and visualizations are polished, clear, and well-designed; story is compelling and well-structured; limitations are thoughtfully acknowledged | Maps and visualizations are clear and adequate; story is coherent; some limitations are noted | Maps and visualizations need significant improvement (missing legends, unclear symbology); story is unclear or disjointed; limitations not addressed | Maps and visualizations are poor or missing key elements; no clear narrative; no acknowledgment of limitations |
| **Organization & Documentation** | Project files are exceptionally well-organized; all data sources are documented with URLs/citations; project summary is clear and complete | Project files are organized logically; most data sources are documented; project summary is present and adequate | Files are somewhat disorganized; documentation is minimal; project summary is incomplete | Files are chaotic and hard to navigate; no documentation of sources; no project summary |

### Scoring Interpretation

**Total possible points:** 24

- **22-24 points (Exemplary):** Exceptional work demonstrating strong GIS skills, clear communication, and professional-level organization. This student is ready for advanced coursework or entry-level GIS work.

- **18-21 points (Proficient):** Solid work showing good understanding of GIS concepts and methods with room to grow. Minor improvements would elevate this to exemplary.

- **14-17 points (Developing):** Adequate work that meets core requirements but has notable gaps in technical execution, analysis depth, or communication. With revision and continued practice, this student can reach proficiency.

- **Below 14 points (Beginning):** Incomplete work requiring significant revision. This student may need additional support or time to develop fundamental skills.

### Additional Assessment Considerations

**Participation components** (if applicable):
- Peer feedback quality (Did they provide thoughtful, specific feedback to classmates?)
- Engagement during presentations (Did they ask questions, take notes, support peers?)
- Reflection quality (Did they engage meaningfully with reflection prompts?)

**Weighting suggestion:**
- Capstone project quality: 70%
- Presentation effectiveness: 20%
- Peer feedback & participation: 10%

**Grading approach:**
- Use the rubric to ensure consistency across students
- Provide written feedback in addition to scores—students benefit from specific guidance
- Consider growth over the semester, not just final product quality
- If a student's project doesn't work due to last-minute technical issues, allow a brief window for resubmission with penalty reduction

## Facilitation Tips

### Keeping Presentations on Time

**Prevention strategies:**
- Require students to submit slides 24 hours in advance for review (if feasible)
- Offer optional practice presentation sessions earlier in the week
- Encourage students to time themselves during practice runs
- Remind them: fewer slides with clear visuals is better than many crowded slides

**During presentations:**
- Use a visible countdown timer so presenters can self-monitor
- Give clear time warnings: 3 minutes left, 1 minute left, "wrap up now"
- If someone consistently exceeds time, adjust Q&A length rather than cutting the next presenter short
- Build buffer time between presentations (1-2 min) to absorb overruns

**When to intervene:**
- If a presenter reaches 11-12 minutes and shows no signs of stopping, gently interrupt: "Thank you—let's pause there and move to questions so we can hear from everyone today."
- If they're on slide 15 of 25 at minute 8, suggest: "Let's jump to your findings slides to make sure we see your key results."
- Frame interventions positively: "You have so much great content—let's make sure we get to your main findings."

**Don't:**
- Let one student take 20 minutes at the expense of others
- Create anxiety by constantly interrupting for time
- Make students feel rushed in a way that undermines their confidence

---

### Encouraging Constructive Questions

**Model good questioning:**
- Ask the first question after each presentation to set the tone
- Use open, curious phrasing: "I'm curious about..." "Can you say more about..." "What led you to..."
- Avoid "gotcha" questions or challenges that feel like critiques
- Balance questions about methods, data, and findings

**Prompt student questions:**
- "What patterns did you notice in [Student's] maps?"
- "Did anyone have a similar challenge in their own project?"
- "What would you want to know more about?"
- If silence: "Turn to a neighbor and share one question, then we'll hear a few."

**Redirect unhelpful questions:**
- If too technical: "That's a great advanced topic—let's connect offline about that."
- If critical: "That's interesting feedback. [Student], how might you approach that differently in a future iteration?"
- If off-topic: "Good question, but let's stay focused on [Student's] research question."

**Celebrate participation:**
- Thank students who ask questions
- Acknowledge insightful observations: "That's a really thoughtful connection to [earlier concept]."

---

### Creating a Celebratory Atmosphere

**Why this matters:**
Capstone presentations can feel stressful and high-stakes. Your job is to create an environment where students feel proud, supported, and celebrated.

**Strategies:**

**1. Verbal affirmation:**
- Start and end with genuine praise
- Note specific strengths in each presentation: "Your use of color to highlight disparities was really effective."
- Use enthusiastic language: "This is excellent work," not "This is fine."

**2. Physical environment:**
- Bring refreshments (cookies, fruit, coffee) if budget allows
- Arrange seating to feel intimate and supportive, not like an exam
- Consider music playing as students arrive to set a positive tone
- Decorate lightly—balloons, a "Congratulations" banner, etc.

**3. Peer support:**
- Encourage applause after each presentation
- Create a space for written kudos (digital board or physical cards where students write compliments)
- Highlight moments when students learn from each other

**4. Acknowledge vulnerability:**
- "Public speaking is hard. Presenting technical work is even harder. You're all doing great."
- Share your own experiences of nervousness or mistakes in presentations
- Normalize imperfection: "Every project has limitations—naming them is a sign of critical thinking."

**5. Capture the moment:**
- Take photos (with permission) during presentations
- Record presentations for students' portfolios
- Create a slideshow of project maps/highlights to show during breaks
- Consider a "Class of [Year]" group photo at the end

---

### Managing Nerves (Yours and Theirs)

**For nervous presenters:**
- Greet students warmly as they set up
- Offer reassurance: "You know this material. Just talk us through what you did."
- Give them a moment to breathe before starting
- Make supportive eye contact during their presentation
- If they freeze or make a mistake, offer gentle support: "Take your time. We're all rooting for you."

**For yourself:**
- You've guided these students all semester—trust that they're prepared
- Have a detailed plan (like this one!) so you're not improvising
- Build in buffer time for the unexpected
- Remember: This is a celebration, not a performance evaluation
- Let students shine—your role is to facilitate, not to be the star

---

### Handling Technical Difficulties

**Common issues and solutions:**

| Problem | Solution |
|---------|----------|
| Screen sharing not working | Have backup: student emails you slides, you present from your screen |
| Slides won't load | Keep PDF backup of all presentations on USB drive |
| Projector fails | Move to a different room or reschedule that segment |
| Student forgot their presentation | If it's in email/cloud, quickly retrieve; otherwise, they present verbally about their project |
| Recording fails | Offer to record makeup session individually later |
| Audio issues (for virtual) | Have student call in via phone; use chat for Q&A |

**Prevention:**
- Test all tech 30+ minutes before start time
- Have IT contact info ready
- Bring backup cables, adapters, dongles
- Download all student presentations in advance if possible
- Communicate backup plan to students ahead of time

**During issues:**
- Stay calm and positive
- Use the time for a break or impromptu discussion
- Don't let tech problems derail the celebration
- Have a sense of humor: "This is why we always have Plan B in GIS!"

## Final Notes for Facilitators

### This is a Celebration

Your tone and energy set the stage. Week 12 should feel like a finish line crossed, not an exam endured. Even if projects are imperfect (and they will be—this is an intro course!), celebrate the growth. Students have moved from complete beginners to people who can conduct spatial analyses, create maps, and communicate findings. That's extraordinary.

### Honor the Journey

Many students will have struggled. Some will have wanted to quit. Some will have felt lost or overwhelmed. The fact that they're here, presenting work they created, is worth acknowledging. Make space for vulnerability and pride to coexist.

### Create Connection

This might be the last time this cohort is together. Help them leave with relationships, not just skills. Encourage exchanging contact info, forming study groups, or meeting up for future GIS events. Community sustains learning better than any textbook.

### Send Them Forward with Confidence

Students will leave with varying levels of proficiency—that's normal. What matters is that they believe they can continue learning, troubleshooting, and growing. Your closing words should emphasize possibility, not perfection. They're at the beginning of their GIS journey, and the path ahead is full of potential.

### Take Care of Yourself

You've guided these students for 12 weeks. You've answered hundreds of questions, debugged countless errors, and provided encouragement through frustration. Take a moment to recognize your own work. Teaching GIS is challenging, and you've done it well.
